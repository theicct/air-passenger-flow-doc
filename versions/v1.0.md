## Executive summary

The T_ijk passenger flow tensor is a calibrated 12×12×12 array of modelled annual air passenger flows at the granularity of the 12 RICE model regions. Each entry T[i, j, k] represents the number of travellers originating in region i who transit through region j en route to destination k. The tensor is the missing calibration input for a Walrasian equilibrium model of aviation tax incidence being developed at the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research (PIK).

Two data sources are combined: the ICCT segment-level emissions inventory for the 2019 baseline year, and the Llano et al. (2023) bilateral tourism panel — a 74-country panel of foreign visitors by residence, air mode, whose most recent vintage in the published dataset is 2018 (i.e. a one-year gap to the 2019 baseline). Routing behaviour is modelled with a two-stage multinomial logit — Stage A determines the direct-versus-one-stop split, and Stage B allocates one-stops across transit regions. Calibration uses both segment-volume and hub-share loss signals, and the tensor totals 784 million inter-region OD passengers plus 2,638 million intra-region OD (grand total 3,423 M) in the 2019 baseline.

### Baseline year

The canonical baseline year is 2019 (pre-COVID). This choice satisfies three constraints simultaneously: (i) it matches the vintage of the Llano et al. panel to within one year (panel data ends at 2018); (ii) it matches Maertens et al. (2020) Table 3 hub transfer rates (also 2018 vintage); (iii) it avoids the Russian airspace closure that began in February 2022 and the still-incomplete China market recovery visible in 2023 data.

A 2023 baseline was tested in early pipeline development but ultimately abandoned in favor of 2019 for the reasons above.

### Calibration frame versus delivery frame

The 12-region definitions above are the canonical Potsdam scheme. Internally, however, the calibration pipeline merges Canada into the USA region and redistributes it back to OHI as a post-processing step. This is because the aggregate OHI region — Canada + Australia + New Zealand + South Korea + Singapore — has structurally bimodal routing patterns (Canada's US-adjacent trans-Atlantic flows versus Asia-Pacific hub flows of the other four), and modelling them together distorts predictions. In the calibration frame, OHI effectively means Asia-Pacific.

## Data inputs

### Llano et al. (2023) bilateral tourism panel

A 74-country, 1995–2018 panel of bilateral visitor flows decomposed by transport mode. We use the total number of non-resident visitors via air (VFR_pf_a) at the 2018 vintage, aggregated to the 12 RICE regions. Units are absolute persons per year.

Three systematic issues require correction before use:

**Air-share representation in the panel.** The panel's mode shares derive from UNWTO transport-mode data, whose coverage of outbound air share varies across origins. The 2018 panel reports CHN at 1.0% air share on outbound international trips, HKG at 4.1%, and a cluster of Eastern European origins (UKR 13%, CZE 24%, ROU 24%) below regional norms. For any origin with international air share below 0.20, we replace it with the median air share of countries in the same RICE region that pass the threshold, and rescale that origin's bilateral air vector by (imputed/observed) — preserving destination structure. 17 origins are imputed; the global air total moves from 862 M (raw) to about 1.05 B (imputed).

**Country-panel scope.** Llano et al. covers 74 of about 200 countries. After aggregation to 12×12 the imputed panel sums to 422 M international air trips, versus an inventory-derived 786 M for 2019. The remaining scope difference is closed via RAS biproportional fitting (§4.3), which scales the panel to match inventory-derived row and column marginals. Countries outside the panel are represented implicitly through the RAS step; the residual scope difference concentrates on EU-arriving flows, AFR-departing/arriving, and OAS-departing/arriving.

**Transoceanic corridors.** For the 51 symmetric region pairs with no land or sea alternative (EU↔USA, EU↔OAS, USA↔CHN, etc.), we substitute the all-mode visitor count for the air-mode count. This corrects a systematic deflation where, for example, UK→USA is reported with 32% air share when it should be close to 100%.

### ICCT 2019 emissions inventory

Segment-level flight records — one row per origin airport × destination airport × airline × aircraft type — with passenger counts. The inventory is used three ways:

- As the source of F_obs[i, k], the inventory-derived 12×12 OD matrix used as RAS marginals (built segment-by-segment, deducting connecting traffic at each segment's arrival airport using published transfer rates).
- As V_obs[a, b], the directed inter-region segment volumes that feed the Stage B segment loss.
- As the disaggregation target for Level-4 segment validation (§6.4).

### Airport transfer rates

Per-region transfer share τ_i and inter-region hub transfer volumes H_obs_inter_i are aggregated from published hub statistics. Sources include the EU top-30 airports panel, Maertens et al. (2020) Table 2 (top-25 global hubs, 2018) and Table 3 (supplementary high-rate hubs), and airport-specific published rates from individual airport authorities.

The published rate τ_i counts every transit passenger at the hub, including those whose feeder or distributor leg is itself intra-region (e.g., LAX-ATL-MIA in ATL's transfer count). For the Stage B target we need only inter-region routing — both legs cross region boundaries. We decompose τ using each airport's inventory-derived intra-region segment share s_a. Under independence between feeder and distributor leg, the share of transfer passengers with both legs inter-region is (1 − s²), and with at least one intra-region leg is s².

Aggregated to regions:

- τ_inter_i ≈ τ_i · (1 − s_i²) — inter-region
- τ_intra_i ≈ τ_i · s_i² — intra-region

### Fare data

The fare signal used in the calibration is a seat-weighted average 2019 economy class fare per RICE region pair, tax-inclusive (purchased from RDC Aviation Ltd.). It is the input to both Stage A (fare_penalty_onestop feature) and Stage B (norm_fare weight) of the MNL. The RDC fare dataset covers 96 of 132 region pairs with ≥10 observations; the remaining pairs use a log-linear distance regression fitted on the same source as a fallback.

## Methodology

### Schedule aggregation and feasible itineraries

Flight schedules (embedded in the ICCT inventory) are aggregated into directed RICE region-pair legs with annual frequency, seat capacity, capacity-weighted distance, block time, and seat-weighted fare.

One-stop itineraries (i→j→k) are enumerated subject to:

- Both legs must operationally exist (freq_ij > 0 and freq_jk > 0).
- No backtracking — first-leg distance < total distance.
- Total flown distance ≤ 1.30 × the direct (i, k) capacity-weighted leg distance, using self-consistent capacity-weighted distances on both sides of the inequality.

Itinerary filtering could be more restrictive and mandate both flight legs to have identical or codeshare airline per an internal Codeshare Dictionary. However, since the T_ijk pipeline operates at coarse RICE region-pair level, the codeshare restriction was deemed unnecessary. A diagnostic test using 2023 inventory and codeshare dictionary finds that only 4.9% of predicted transit volume would be excluded under a strict filter, with most of the exclusion concentrated on RUS-transit routings attributable to post-2022 sanctions. The remaining exclusion is also arguably an overestimate: a growing share of passengers self-connect on separate tickets, so real-world one-stop flows include some itineraries that fail a strict airline codeshare test.

Connecting fares are constructed as summed segment fares scaled by a per-transit-region multiplier that encodes the nonstop premium that segment-level fares would miss: MDE 0.60 (Gulf sixth-freedom undercut), AFR 0.65 (Ethiopian ADD hub), OAS 0.70 (intra-Asia LCC + Singapore), default 0.80 (Brueckner 2003 alliance discount). One origin-specific override applies: (LAM → MDE) = 0.80, reflecting that non-Brazil LAM does not route via the Gulf in practice.

### Two-stage routing model

Routing share share[i, j, k] is decomposed into a binary direct-versus-one-stop logit (Stage A) and a conditional MNL over transit choice given one-stop (Stage B).

**Stage A** — binary logit on direct share with 6-vector θ:

```
logit P(direct | i, k) = θ₀ + θ₁ log_seats_direct + θ₂ log_freq_direct
                            + θ₃ time_penalty_onestop + θ₄ fare_penalty_onestop
                            + θ₅ log_dist_ik
```

**Stage B** — conditional MNL over transit:

```
w(i→j→k) = exp(−α · total_time) · exp(−β · norm_fare) · bottleneck_seats^γ
share_onestop[i, j, k] = w / Σⱼ w
```

norm_fare is the one-stop's fare divided by the cheapest one-stop fare in (i, k).

Combined:

```
share[i, i, k] = P(direct | i, k)                        (j=i sentinel = 'direct')
share[i, j, k] = (1 − P(direct)) · share_onestop[i, j, k]   for j ≠ i, k
```

### F_obs construction

F_obs is built from the Llano et al. panel in three steps: (i) air-share imputation (§3.1), (ii) transoceanic all-mode override for physically-air-only corridors (§3.1), and (iii) RAS biproportional fitting to inventory-derived row and column marginals from the 2019 OD matrix. The 2019 OD matrix used as RAS reference is itself built from the segment-level inventory by applying a per-segment (1 − τ_arrival) deduction at every airport pair — pax(a → b) × (1 − τ_b) — to strip out the connecting-pax fraction and estimate true origin-destination demand on that leg. These segment-level OD estimates are then aggregated to region pairs to form the row/column marginals. Both sides of the RAS therefore arrive at the fitting step already at OD scale: Llano et al. VFR_pf_a counts destination-country arrivals by residence (no transit double-counting by construction), and the inventory F_obs marginals have the per-airport τ deduction baked in upstream. This is why the RAS itself does not apply a further (1 − τ) at the region level — doing so would double-count the deduction, an issue that collapsed AFR/LAM/RUS row/col targets when high-τ Table 3 hubs (ADD, PTY, SVO) entered the panel in 2026-05.

Mass evolution: the pre-RAS imputed Llano et al. panel sums to 422 M inter-region trips; RAS scales it to 786 M to match inventory row/col sums. The diagonal is forced to zero, and off-diagonal seeds use a 1.0-passenger floor.

### Tensor assembly and Canada split

The inter-region tensor is computed on the calibration frame as:

```
T[i, j, k] = F_obs[i, k] · share[i, j, k]
```

In the calibration frame, Canada and the United States are combined into an artificial North America region to approximate real-world aviation market dynamics. Canadian flows are then redistributed back into OHI in the delivery frame using inventory-derived CA-vs-US shares per axis:

- **Origin axis on outbound NAM:** p_origin_ca[k] = share of NAM → k segment pax originating in Canada.
- **Destination axis on inbound NAM:** p_dest_ca[i] = share of i → NAM inbound landing in Canada.
- **Transit axis on true one-stops** (j = USA, j ≠ i): geometric-mean proxy √(p_origin_ca · p_dest_ca).
- **Direct sentinels** (j = i) follow the origin assignment.

Mass is exactly conserved across the split.

### Intra-region OD diagonal

The intra-region OD (T[i, i, i]) is the largest single component of the tensor — roughly 2.64 B passengers across the 12 diagonal cells, versus 0.77 B inter-region OD.

For each region i:

```
T[i, i, i] = inventory_intra_seg[i] · (1 − τ_i)
```

The (1 − τ) deflation backs out segment passengers to OD passengers by removing connecting traffic counted twice in segment volume. Raw τ is used here (not τ_inter) because ALL transit at intra-region hubs — whether the onward leg is intra- or inter-region — must be subtracted to yield true intra-region OD demand. This is the opposite of the Stage B use case (§4.3) where τ_inter is needed to remove only inter-region transit.

## Calibration

θ is fit first, then (α, γ) — β is held fixed at 0.6 throughout.

### Stage A target with V/F deflation

For each (i, k) with both direct and one-stop alternatives and positive F_obs:

```
target_p[i, k] = V_obs[i, k] · (1 − τ_inter_i)(1 − τ_inter_k) / F_obs[i, k]
```

clipped to [0.05, 0.95].

V_obs[i, k] is segment passengers on the i→k nonstop leg (which includes inter-region transit passengers using the leg). The (1 − τ_inter_i)(1 − τ_inter_k) factor backs out direct OD by removing the share of segment passengers who are inter-region transit at either endpoint. The undeflated V/F target has median 0.93 and 47% of pairs exceed 1.0 — meaning segment passengers exceed OD demand for nearly half of markets — which without deflation biases Stage A toward a direct-dominant solution.

Fitting is by weighted least squares in log-odds space (weights = F_obs).

**Stage A fit:** θ = (6.163, −2.090, 2.878, 0.921, −0.848, −0.221); loss = 1.115; n_fit = 72 markets.

### Stage B loss

Segment volumes alone leave α and γ pinned to bounds because they only identify within-one-stop substitution, not the direct-versus-one-stop split. The Stage B loss therefore blends two signals with equal weightings:

- **Segment loss** — mean squared log-difference between predicted and observed directed inter-region leg volumes V_obs, restricted to pairs with V_obs ≥ 1000.
- **Hub-share loss** — per covered region j, predicted transfer volume Σ F_obs[i, k] · share[i, j, k] compared to H_obs_inter share across covered regions in log-share space. Comparing shares (not levels) cancels the hub-coverage bias.

**Stage B fit:** α = 0.005 (lower bound), γ = 0.757. Blended loss = 1.229 (segment = 1.560, hub-share = 0.897).

### β fixed at 0.6

β is fixed at 0.6 rather than fitted jointly with (α, γ). Free-fit β is not viable: fare and total-time features are strongly collinear across the one-stop itinerary set (Pearson r = 0.955 pooled; within-market median r ≈ +0.68), producing a ridge in the (α, β) likelihood surface that the optimizer resolves by pushing β to whichever bound it walks into.

To pick a defensible fixed value, we anchored the sensitivity grid to the airline-itinerary-choice MNL literature range. Lurkin et al. (2017) report uncorrected fare coefficients of −0.0025 to −0.0049 per dollar (high-yield versus low-yield segments), corresponding to β ∈ [0.75, 1.47] in our normalisation with norm_fare = total_fare / min_fare_in_(i, k) and typical min fare ≈ USD 300; endogeneity-corrected estimates are 40–70% larger, spanning β ∈ [1.08, 2.07]. Two anchor points below this band (β = 0.3, 0.6) were added because the literature values are almost entirely estimated on US domestic short-to-medium haul markets, while the T_ijk tensor is dominated by long-haul international corridors where fare sensitivity is empirically lower: long-haul demand is a mix of business travellers with weakly elastic price response and leisure travellers who book further in advance under stable published fares, so a lower β is plausibly the right prior in this regime. One anchor above the band (β = 3.0) was retained to bracket the previous canonical value.

With (α, γ) refitted at each grid point, blended loss decreases monotonically as β falls (1.42 → 1.33 → 1.26 → 1.24 → 1.23 → 1.23); β = 0.3 is the loss minimum within the swept range, with β = 0.6 essentially tied (Δloss < 0.3%). We adopt **β = 0.6** as the canonical value — the conservative choice that sits just below the uncorrected low edge (β_dollar ≈ −0.002 per dollar; 80% of the Lurkin high-yield uncorrected magnitude) rather than the sweep-minimum β = 0.3 (β_dollar ≈ −0.001 per dollar; 40% of that edge). The haul-composition argument justifies sitting below the range but not by an arbitrary amount, and β = 0.6 keeps the model within a factor of ~1.25 of a directly reported literature value while capturing the haul-mix adjustment. The uncorrected literature band (rather than the endogeneity-corrected band) is the reference because our estimator, like Lurkin's before their instrument was applied, has no price-endogeneity correction.

Sensitivity analysis confirms that all top-20 tensor cells (~90% of inter-region OD mass) are exactly invariant to β within [0.3, 3.0]; only the routing distribution across single-hub regions like RUS varies materially (≈2.2× across the swept range).

### α at lower bound

α converges to its lower bound of 0.005/hr, far below the −0.30/hr time coefficient reported by Lurkin et al. (2017) for a linear-time airline itinerary-choice MNL — the most directly comparable published value. To test whether α is genuinely near zero or pinned by a bound artefact, we held α at literature values corresponding to representative values of time (USD 15/hr → α = 0.15, USD 25/hr → α = 0.25, USD 40/hr → α = 0.40) and re-optimised γ (with β held at its canonical fixed value; the qualitative pattern is invariant to reasonable β choices). Blended loss rises monotonically from 1.23 at the bound to above 2.0 at α = 0.4, with both segment and hub-share losses worsening. Per-region transit rebalances toward geographically direct hubs (RUS transit grows from ≈1.3 M to ≈8 M at USD 25/hr VoT; EUR from ≈2.5 M to ≈4.4 M) and away from the empirically dominant sixth-freedom hubs (MDE drops from ≈27 M to ≈23 M). Forcing literature-consistent time sensitivity pulls the model in the wrong direction relative to the observed hub-share targets.

The literature H_obs_inter targets show MDE at lit_τ_inter = 0.51 (highest of any region), meaning that in the observed data real routing accepts substantial geographic detour when carriers offer competitive frequency, price, and schedule alignment. This structural signal survives in the calibration only when α is small; forcing α up erodes it. The α = 0.005 outcome is therefore what the joint calibration objective requires given the data available at this aggregation scale, not evidence that travellers do not value time.

The implied value of time at the canonical (α, β) = (0.005, 0.6) is roughly USD 2.50/hr — an order of magnitude below leisure literature (USD 10–25/hr) and two orders of magnitude below business estimates (USD 30–60/hr). This is best read as a signature of the aggregation scale rather than a preference-parameter estimate. At region-pair granularity one traveller's fast-preference and another's cheap-preference average out; the residual signal is dominated by capacity (which is why γ identifies cleanly) and by fare only weakly. Any fixed β leaves the implied VoT dominated by α — and α at this scale is driven toward zero by the calibration objective regardless of β. Recovering a literature-consistent VoT would require both a larger α (≈ 0.30/hr per Lurkin et al. 2017) and per-carrier fare data to break the α–β collinearity; neither is achievable at region-pair aggregation without breaking the routing fit. Value of time for downstream economic models should therefore be sourced from the published literature rather than back-solved from our fitted α.

### Level 4 — segment-level RMSLE

The tensor is disaggregated to directed inter-region segment volumes V[a, b] and redistributed to airport-pair segments within (a, b) proportional to seat capacity. Intra-region segments receive pred = 0 by construction (the model is inter-region only).

RMSLE is defined as sqrt(mean((log1p(pred) − log1p(obs))²)).

### Level 5 — qualitative Google Flights audit

27 OD pairs across 12 representative region pairs were queried on Google Flights (default ranking). The audit confirms the operational transit set — which hubs appear as bookable connections for a given corridor — but cannot be read as a passenger-share estimate (Google's ranking is price plus convenience, not volume). Two vintage caveats: US-China direct collapsed post-COVID, and Russian airspace closed since February 2022, so any RUS transit on Europe-Asia or trans-Pacific routes is operationally impossible in the current schedule snapshot but was in service in 2019. The audit informed several calibration choices, including the LAM→MDE connecting-fare discount override.

## Future work

The following items are areas where the model could be strengthened in a future release.

### Data coverage extensions

**Tourism panel's African coverage.** The Llano et al. 2018 panel includes 5 of ~54 African countries (DZA, LBY, MAR, TUN, ZAF); most of West, East, and Central Africa are outside the panel. A next iteration could supplement the tourism panel with an auxiliary bilateral source (e.g. UN DESA migrant-stock × visitation rates) for major missing African origins.

**RUS and EUR inventory coverage.** The 2019 inventory used for F_obs marginals under-counts RUS↔EU segments (0.14 M observed versus an estimated 5 M+ reality) because Aeroflot / S7 / Rossiya trunks to EU capitals are absent from the provider's data, and Ukraine (KBP) is similarly under-counted.

### Model specification refinements

**Per-transit alternative-specific constants (ASC) in Stage A.** The Stage A fare-penalty coefficient θ₄ currently carries an economically wrong sign because on nearly all high-volume corridors the cheapest connecting fare undercuts the direct fare (sixth-freedom / alliance discount) yet P(direct) remains high due to a nonstop premium. A diagnostic replication with per-transit ASCs — the standard airline-itinerary-choice literature specification — reduces Stage A WLS loss by 18% and produces interpretable per-transit-region coefficients consistent with sixth-freedom hub economics. Downstream L4 validation metrics are essentially unchanged (RMSLE 0.706 versus 0.707), so this refinement is documented as a diagnostic but not adopted for v1.0. A future version aimed at a methods paper would benefit from adopting the ASC specification for interpretability.

**Corridor-specific connecting-fare discounts.** The current per-transit-region connecting-fare discount table (MDE 0.60, AFR 0.65, OAS 0.70, default 0.80) is qualitative midpoints from airline pricing literature. A future version could fit these as parameters against direct-share residuals on Gulf-touched corridors.
